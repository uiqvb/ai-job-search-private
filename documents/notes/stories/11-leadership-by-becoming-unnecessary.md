# 11. Leadership by Becoming Unnecessary

**Hook:** I have not been a club president. What I can show you is that in every role I have
held, the work ended with someone else able to do it without me.

**Used in:** Deakin Student Experience Assistant, which is a role that explicitly asks about
leadership.

**Best for:** any leadership or supervision question where the honest answer is that I do not
have a titled leadership role.

---

## The move

Refuse the premise politely, then redefine the evidence. "I will give you the honest version
rather than the impressive one." Then give three examples where the measure of leadership is
handover, not authority.

## The three examples

**7-Eleven.** Trained new starters on the POS, food safety and shift routines, while running
shifts solo and opening and closing the store unsupervised.

> Training someone on a till during peak trade teaches you quickly that nobody learns from being
> talked through the whole process at once. They learn by doing one part of it while you stand
> close enough to catch it and far enough back that the customer is still theirs.

**Boroondara Stroke Support Group.** Ran the monthly activity calendar publication for 12 months,
then trained new volunteers to run it themselves. The whole point was that it should stop
depending on me.

**Deakin Software Engineering Club.** Built the application behind the debugging workshop and
designed the defects, then floated the room as a helper. Having written every bug myself, I knew
where people would get stuck. The job was to get them unstuck without reaching over and taking
the keyboard, which is harder than it sounds and is most of what supporting a team actually is.
See `04-designing-the-defects.md`.

## The four operational commitments

The Assistant letter converts the story into a plan for running the Ambassador team. These are
worth keeping because they are specific enough to be argued with, which is what makes them
credible:

- **Brief before the shift, not during it.** Most nerves on a stall are not about confidence,
  they are about not knowing what you are going to be asked. Ten minutes at the start on the five
  questions we will genuinely get that day is worth more than any amount of encouragement.
- **Take the first hard question, then hand the second one back.** Answering everything keeps
  someone dependent on you all day. Modelling it once then stepping back is how they finish the
  shift able to handle it alone.
- **Watch for the third hour.** Orientation shifts are long and there is a predictable point
  where people go quiet and start letting students walk past. Rotating someone off the front for
  twenty minutes is not coddling them, it is how you get a good fourth hour.
- **Name what went well, out loud, at the end.** Casual staff on a 100-hour-a-year contract
  rarely hear specifically what they did well. It costs nothing and it is the thing most likely
  to bring someone back for the next intake.

## What it evidences

- Leadership defined as capability transfer rather than status
- Concrete teaching technique, observed rather than theorised
- Operational thinking about a shift, not just enthusiasm for the role
