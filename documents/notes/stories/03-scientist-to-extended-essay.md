# 03. The Scientist and the Extended Essay

**Hook:** I wanted to be a scientist as a kid. I never grew out of the reason why.

**Used in:** CSIRO Final (the whole letter is built on this).

**Best for:** research roles, honours and PhD pathways, anything asking why research, motivation
and long-horizon focus.

---

## The story

- As a child I wanted to be a scientist without understanding what that meant. It meant finding
  experiments on YouTube, gathering whatever was around the house, and trying them. Sometimes
  they worked, sometimes they did not. Either way I loved changing something, watching what
  happened, and feeling as though I had discovered something myself.
- I stopped telling people I wanted to be a scientist. I do not think I ever grew out of the
  reason I wanted to be one.
- I felt it again in Year 12, during my IB Psychology Extended Essay. One question, months to
  work on it, and a body of research I had to read, understand and turn into a conclusion I was
  willing to defend.
- I became completely absorbed. I would follow one paper into another, find something that
  challenged what I thought I understood, go backwards, reconsider, and slowly make the pieces
  fit.
- I received an **A** for the essay. What stayed with me was not the grade. It was spending
  months thinking deeply about essentially one question and enjoying that.
- There are not many things that make me lose track of time. Being completely invested in one
  problem does.
- Years later I found the same feeling in engineering, which is where story 01 picks up.

## The pattern this story sets up

The CSIRO Final letter uses this to draw an explicit line:

> The child copying science experiments from YouTube, the Year 12 student buried in psychology
> literature, and the engineering student refusing to leave a broken robot alone were doing very
> different things. What I enjoyed underneath them was remarkably similar.

And then names it: I like finding solutions, testing whether an idea is actually true,
experimenting and seeing what happens when an assumption meets reality, building things,
breaking them, understanding why they broke, and trying again.

## What it evidences

- Genuine interest in research as a process rather than as a job title
- Capacity to stay with a single question for months
- Comfort with being wrong and following evidence backwards

## The honesty move that makes it work

The letter does not claim research is the answer. It says the opposite:

> I do not want to pretend that I already know research is the career for me. I do not.

And it names what real research requires that I have not done: rigorous methodology, careful
documentation, analysis, reproducibility, responsible experimental design. Disciplines I have
only begun to experience. Keep this. Removing it turns the story into a claim I cannot support.

## Length variants

**One line.** I wanted to be a scientist as a child, found the same feeling again in an IB
Psychology Extended Essay that I spent months on, and I want to find out whether that instinct
holds up in an environment where research is the actual work.

**Short.** As a kid I copied science experiments off YouTube because I liked changing something
and watching what happened. I found that again in Year 12 doing my IB Psychology Extended Essay,
where I spent months on a single question and received an A. What stayed with me was not the
grade, it was that I enjoyed thinking about one problem for that long.
